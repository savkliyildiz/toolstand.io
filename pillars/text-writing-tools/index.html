Text Analysis & Statistics: Know Your Writing

Before you can improve your writing, you need to understand it. ToolStand's analysis tools give you the hard numbers — word counts, readability scores, and even LLM token estimates — so you can make data-driven decisions about your text.

Word & Character Counter

The Word & Character Counter is the foundation of the entire text toolkit. Paste or type any text, and it updates in real time: total words, characters (with and without spaces), sentences, paragraphs, and estimated reading time. The reading time is calculated using the industry-standard 238 words-per-minute average for English-language silent reading, making it useful for estimating how long a blog post, speech, or article will take to consume.

Why does characters-without-spaces matter? If you are writing for platforms with character limits — Twitter/X posts, meta descriptions, SMS messages, app store descriptions — the without-spaces count is often what the platform enforces (spaces are free). The sentence and paragraph counters are equally practical: a high sentence count with a low paragraph count suggests wall-of-text formatting that may intimidate readers, while the paragraph count helps you assess document structure at a glance.

The counter handles large documents efficiently. A 50,000-word manuscript processes in under a second, and the real-time update means you can watch your word count climb as you type, which is surprisingly motivating when you are pushing toward a daily writing goal.

Readability Checker

The Readability Checker is where the toolkit gets genuinely powerful. Paste your text, and it computes five industry-standard readability scores simultaneously:

  • Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level: The U.S. school grade needed to understand the text. A score of 8.0 means an eighth-grader should comprehend it. Most web content targets grades 7–9.
  • Flesch Reading Ease: A 0–100 scale where higher is easier. Scores of 60–70 are considered "plain English" and ideal for general audiences. Legal documents often score below 30.
  • Gunning Fog Index: Estimates the years of formal education needed to understand the text on first reading. A score above 12 indicates the text is difficult for most readers.
  • SMOG Index: (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook) Another grade-level estimate, commonly used in healthcare to ensure patient materials are accessible.
  • Coleman-Liau Index: Unlike the syllable-based formulas, this one uses character count instead, making it particularly useful for evaluating texts where syllable counting may be unreliable.

The checker also provides automated suggestions: it identifies sentences that exceed recommended length thresholds, flags complex words that could be simplified, and highlights passive voice constructions. This turns the readability checker from a passive score card into an active editing assistant. For SEO writers, Flesch Reading Ease correlates with search rankings — Google's algorithms increasingly reward content that is accessible to a broad audience.

One practical tip: run the readability checker twice. Once early in your draft to identify structural problems (sentences that are too long, paragraphs that are too dense), and once on your final draft to confirm your scores are in the target range. The difference between the two readings tells you how much your editing improved the text.

Token Counter

The Token Counter addresses a uniquely 2026 concern: how much of an LLM's context window will your text consume? Using a statistical approximation of OpenAI's cl100k_base tokenizer (used by GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo), the counter estimates token counts with roughly ±10% accuracy.

This matters for several practical reasons. If you are building a prompt that includes a large document as context, the token counter tells you whether it will fit within GPT-4's 128K context window — or whether you need to trim. It also estimates API cost based on current per-token pricing, so you know what each API call will cost before you make it. For developers integrating LLMs into their applications, the counter is an essential planning tool that prevents expensive surprises.

Text Transformation Tools: Reshape Your Content

Raw text rarely arrives in the format you need. Maybe you wrote everything in lowercase during a brainstorming session and now need proper title case. Maybe you need placeholder text for a design mockup. Maybe you need to convert a numeric value into words for a cheque or legal document. ToolStand's transformation tools handle all of these conversions instantly.

Case Converter

The Case Converter supports ten case formats that cover every writing and programming scenario:

  • UPPERCASE: EVERY CHARACTER CAPITALIZED. Useful for headings, acronyms, or when a style guide demands it.
  • lowercase: all characters in lowercase. Essential when normalizing user input or preparing text for case-insensitive comparison.
  • Title Case: Every Major Word Starts with a Capital. Follows AP-style rules (articles and short prepositions remain lowercase).
  • Sentence case: Only the first word and proper nouns are capitalized. The standard for body text in most languages.
  • camelCase: first word lowercase, subsequent words capitalized with no spaces. The convention for JavaScript variable names.
  • PascalCase: Every Word Capitalized Without Spaces. Used for C# and Java class names, React component names.
  • snake_case: all_lowercase_with_underscores. Python variable and function names, database column names.
  • kebab-case: all-lowercase-with-hyphens. CSS class names, URL slugs, HTML attributes.
  • CONSTANT_CASE: ALL_UPPERCASE_WITH_UNDERSCORES. Used for environment variables and configuration constants.
  • tOGGLE cASE: iNVERTS tHE cASE oF eVERY cHARACTER. Occasionally useful for stylistic effect or fixing caps-lock accidents.

What makes this converter genuinely useful is that it handles edge cases intelligently. Converting "iPhone 15 Pro" to lowercase produces "iphone 15 pro" — not "iphone 15 pro" with the original capital P. Title case recognizes that "the," "a," "an," "in," "on," "at," "for," "and," "but," "or," and "nor" should remain lowercase unless they are the first or last word. The converter also strips extra whitespace and normalizes line endings, so you don't end up with formatting artifacts after conversion.

Security & Encryption: Write Without Fear

Some text should not live in plain sight. Source notes, proprietary business plans, personal journal entries, or simply a password you need to send to a colleague — these all demand encryption. ToolStand's security tools bring military-grade protection to your browser.

Text Encryptor / Decryptor

The Text Encryptor uses AES-256-GCM encryption via the browser-native Web Crypto API. Here is what that means in practice: you type or paste text, set a password, and click Encrypt. The tool derives an encryption key from your password using PBKDF2 (with 100,000 iterations of SHA-256), then encrypts the text with AES-256 in Galois/Counter Mode. The output is a Base64-encoded ciphertext that includes the encrypted data, the PBKDF2 salt, and the GCM initialization vector — everything needed for decryption by someone who knows the password.

Critically, your text never leaves your browser. The Web Crypto API operations execute entirely in the browser's JavaScript runtime. No server receives your plaintext, your ciphertext, or your password. This is fundamentally different from "encrypted messaging" services where the server facilitates key exchange — here, there is no server involvement at all. Share the encrypted output via email, messaging apps, or any insecure channel; only someone with the password can decrypt it.

AES-256-GCM provides both confidentiality (no one can read the text without the key) and integrity (any tampering with the ciphertext is detected and decryption will fail). The 256-bit key means there are 2^256 possible keys — a number so large that brute-forcing it is physically impossible with current or foreseeable computing technology, including quantum computers.

Password Strength Checker

The Password Strength Checker evaluates passwords by calculating their entropy — the mathematical measure of unpredictability. A password with 60+ bits of entropy is considered strong; 80+ bits is very strong; 128+ bits is overkill for most purposes. The checker also estimates brute-force cracking time based on realistic attack speeds (billions of guesses per second for offline attacks against fast hashes, thousands per second for online attacks with rate limiting).

Beyond entropy, the checker identifies common patterns that weaken passwords: dictionary words, keyboard walks ("qwerty"), sequential characters ("12345"), repeated characters ("aaaa"), and context-specific patterns like dates or names. It also checks against a database of known compromised passwords (without transmitting your password — the check uses a k-anonymity model where only a partial hash prefix is sent).

Password Generator

The Password Generator creates strong random passwords with customizable parameters: length (up to 128 characters), character sets (uppercase, lowercase, numbers, symbols), and an option to exclude ambiguous characters (I/l/1, O/0) that cause confusion in some fonts. A "pronounceable" mode generates passwords like "bafomika" that are easier to remember while maintaining reasonable entropy. Every generated password is created using the browser's cryptographically secure random number generator (crypto.getRandomValues), not a pseudo-random algorithm.

The Complete Writing Workflow: Draft → Check → Format → Encrypt/Share

Individually, each of these tools solves a specific problem. But their real power emerges when you chain them together into a complete writing workflow. Here is a step-by-step example of writing a blog post from first draft to publication using only ToolStand tools.

Step 1: Draft. Write your initial draft however you normally would — in a text editor, Google Docs, or directly in the Word Counter if you want real-time statistics as you write. Do not edit while drafting; just get words on the page. Aim for your target word count (the counter keeps you honest).

Step 2: Check readability. Take your completed draft and paste it into the Readability Checker. Look at all five scores. If your Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level is above 12 and you are writing for a general audience, you have work to do. Note the specific sentences flagged as too long or too complex — these are your editing targets.

Step 3: Edit and re-check. Rewrite the flagged sentences. Shorten them. Replace complex words with simpler alternatives. Run the readability checker again. You should see your scores improve. Iterate until you hit your target grade level. (For web content, grades 7–9 are ideal; for technical documentation, grades 10–12 are acceptable.)

Step 4: Format for your platform. If your blog uses Markdown, paste the edited text into the Markdown Previewer and add formatting — headers, bold, italics, links, code blocks. Use the Markdown Table Generator if your post includes any tables. Preview the rendered output to confirm everything looks right. If your CMS uses HTML, use the HTML Entity Encoder to safely embed any code examples.

Step 5: Convert case if needed. Need your title in Title Case? Run it through the Case Converter. Need your URL slug in kebab-case? The case converter handles that too. Consistent casing across your post's metadata is a small detail that makes a big difference in perceived professionalism.

Step 6: Diff revisions. If you worked with an editor or made multiple revision passes, use the Diff Checker to compare versions. This is especially useful for confirming that you accepted all the editor's suggestions and did not accidentally revert any changes.

Step 7: Encrypt sensitive content (if applicable). If your post references proprietary data, includes embargoed information, or you are sharing a draft with a co-author over an insecure channel, use the Text Encryptor to encrypt the document before sharing. Set a strong password (use the Password Generator to create one, and the Password Strength Checker to verify its entropy), then share the password through a separate channel — Signal, a phone call, or in person.

Step 8: Share and publish. With your text formatted, readability-optimized, and (if needed) encrypted, you are ready to publish or share. Every step happened in your browser. No tool saw your content. No server processed your data. Your ideas remained yours, end to end.

This workflow works for more than blog posts. Developers writing documentation follow the same steps: draft in the word counter, check readability of API docs (yes, documentation should be readable!), preview in the Markdown previewer, diff versions after code review. Students follow it for essays: draft, check readability and word count, format citations, diff the final version against the rough draft to confirm all edits are intentional. Privacy & Security Deep Dive: Why Client-Side Matters

The defining feature of ToolStand's text tools is that they run entirely in your browser. This is not a marketing claim — it is an architectural decision with real privacy and security implications.

How AES-256-GCM encryption works in the browser. When you use the text encryptor, the browser's Web Crypto API handles every cryptographic operation. The process works like this: (1) You provide a password. (2) PBKDF2 derives a 256-bit encryption key from your password using 100,000 iterations of SHA-256, combined with a randomly generated 128-bit salt. This makes brute-force attacks against the password computationally expensive. (3) A random 96-bit initialization vector (IV) is generated — this ensures that encrypting the same text with the same password twice produces different ciphertext. (4) AES-256-GCM encrypts the text and produces an authentication tag that verifies the ciphertext has not been tampered with. (5) The salt, IV, and ciphertext are packaged into a single Base64-encoded output.

All five steps execute in the browser's JavaScript runtime. The Web Crypto API is implemented by the browser vendor (Google, Mozilla, Apple, Microsoft) and is subject to the same security auditing as the browser's TLS implementation. This is safer than using a third-party JavaScript crypto library that may have implementation bugs or, worse, be a malicious copy.

Why client-side text processing matters. Consider what happens when you use common writing tools: Google Docs uploads every keystroke to Google's servers. Grammarly sends your text to its servers for grammar and style analysis — and its privacy policy permits using your content to train its AI models. The Hemingway App desktop version keeps things local, but the web version processes text server-side. Even tools marketed as "private" may send analytics data, error logs, or "improvement data" that includes snippets of your text.

ToolStand's architecture eliminates all of these risks. The text you paste into the word counter, the readability checker, the diff checker, or the encryptor remains in your browser's memory. When you close the tab, it is gone. No server logs. No analytics capturing your content. No "we may use your data to improve our services" clause buried in a privacy policy.

This matters for several categories of writers in particular. Journalists handling sensitive source material cannot risk their notes appearing on a third-party server. Business professionals working on proprietary strategies or financial projections need assurance that their drafts are not stored or analyzed. Healthcare writers dealing with patient-adjacent content may have HIPAA considerations. Legal professionals drafting privileged documents cannot ethically use tools that store or transmit content. And any writer who simply values the principle that their unfinished, unpolished drafts are nobody else's business benefits from a client-side only approach.

Comparison at a glance: Google Docs stores everything in the cloud, accessible to Google. Grammarly transmits text to servers for analysis and may use it for AI training. Hemingway App (web) processes text server-side. ToolStand processes nothing on any server — your browser does all the work, and your text stays on your device.

The trade-off is that ToolStand cannot offer multi-device sync or collaborative editing — those features inherently require server-side storage. But for the solo writing workflow — draft, analyze, format, encrypt, share — the privacy guarantee is worth the trade-off for anyone who takes their content's confidentiality seriously.

What is AES-256 encryption and is it secure?

AES-256 (Advanced Encryption Standard with a 256-bit key) is a symmetric encryption algorithm adopted by the U.S. government and used worldwide for securing classified information up to the TOP SECRET level. AES-256-GCM — the specific mode used by ToolStand's text encryptor — provides both confidentiality (no one can read the text) and integrity (any tampering is detected). The character counter includes both with-spaces and without-spaces counts, which is especially useful for languages with different spacing conventions. For accurate CJK word counting, specialized tools using morphological analysis are recommended — ToolStand's counter is optimized for Latin-script languages.

What's the difference between Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level and Reading Ease?

The Flesch Reading Ease score runs from 0–100, where higher scores mean easier reading. Scores of 90–100 indicate a 5th-grade reading level (very easy), 60–70 indicate 8th–9th grade (plain English, ideal for general audiences), and 0–30 indicate college graduate level (very difficult, like academic papers or legal documents). The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level is the mathematical inverse, outputting a U.S. school grade level directly — a score of 8.5 means an 8th grader should comprehend the text. Both use the same underlying formula based on average sentence length and syllables per word, but express the result differently: Reading Ease is a scale, Grade Level is a label. Most writers find Grade Level more intuitive ("I want this at an 8th-grade level"), while Reading Ease is better for tracking incremental improvements.

For optimal performance, documents up to approximately 50,000 characters (roughly 8,000–10,000 words) are processed nearly instantly in modern browsers. Very large documents (100,000+ characters) may cause a brief delay of a few seconds while the diff is computed. How does the token counter estimate LLM tokens?

The token counter provides an approximation based on the cl100k_base tokenizer used by GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo models. It applies a statistical model that accounts for how OpenAI's tokenizer handles different types of text: common English words typically use 1 token each, unusual words and technical terms use 2–3 tokens, whitespace and punctuation each consume tokens, and non-English text tends to use more tokens per word. The estimate is typically within ±10% of actual API token counts. The tool also shows the estimated API cost based on current GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo per-token pricing, and indicates what percentage of the model's context window your text occupies — essential for planning prompts that need to fit within context limits.
